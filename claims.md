# Six arguments in *Arm’s Length AI*

This file follows the structure and language of [`survey-analysis.md`](survey-analysis.md). It is not a separate theory of the survey.

Read [`approved-findings.md`](approved-findings.md) alongside this file. The approved findings are the evidence layer. The text below identifies the argument each group of findings supports, and the question it opens for a reader.

## 1. The distrust thesis

**Article section:** [The distrust thesis](survey-analysis.md#the-distrust-thesis)  
**Relevant findings:** F01, F03, F11

**Survey finding:** AI is routine in the surveyed cohort, but only around one quarter trust its output enough to support critical decisions. Around three fifths identify decisions made on inaccurate, partial or hallucinated information as a potential negative impact.

**Argument in the article:** Fashion is not standing outside the AI wave. It is using AI daily while keeping it at a safe distance when the work becomes consequential. The article calls that gulf in trust both a symptom and an ongoing cause of AI’s hesitant relationship with fashion.

**What remains uncertain:** The survey records confidence and concern. It does not establish why a particular user, team or organisation does not trust an AI system.

**Question to test:** Which important decisions are people unwilling to base on AI output, and what would make those outputs more useful, grounded and verifiable?

## 2. The shape of fashion’s AI estate

**Article section:** [The shape of fashion’s AI estate](survey-analysis.md#the-shape-of-fashions-ai-estate)  
**Relevant findings:** F02, F05, F06

**Survey finding:** Around two thirds bring personal AI tools into work, and around one quarter do so without permission. Around two thirds describe AI initiatives as sandboxed or standalone tools with limited connections to other systems. Few report a good method for measuring AI return on investment.

**Argument in the article:** Fashion does not have one AI stack. It has personal tools, sanctioned products, embedded features, local habits and different payment models. The resulting estate is difficult to see, govern and price as a coherent system.

**What remains uncertain:** The companion cannot see an organisation’s actual tool estate, contracts, data flows, controls or costs.

**Question to test:** Can we name each AI surface in use, who owns it, what it sees, what it costs, which workflow it affects and where human review happens?

## 3. Opaque opex and rough yardsticks for ROI

**Article section:** [Opaque opex and rough yardsticks for ROI](survey-analysis.md#opaque-opex-and-rough-yardsticks-for-roi)  
**Relevant findings:** F04, F06, F07

**Survey finding:** Around three quarters say their organisation has expanded AI activity in the past year. Around two thirds expect AI spending to increase, and expanding use or price rises within existing projects were cited more often than new projects. Few report a good ROI method, while around two fifths have seen an AI initiative fail.

**Argument in the article:** The relevant spending story is not simply that fashion is funding new AI ideas. Organisations may be spending more to provision, support and realise value from work already underway. That becomes difficult to judge when projects aim at broad outcomes such as productivity or efficiency without a reliable yardstick.

**What remains uncertain:** The survey does not show whether a particular tool is overpriced, whether an investment will fail, or which pricing model produces better outcomes.

**Question to test:** Can we show the full cost and intended return of one live AI workflow, including licences, usage, integration, training, review and failure handling?

## 4. Maturity everywhere but the middle

**Article section:** [Maturity everywhere but the middle](survey-analysis.md#maturity-everywhere-but-the-middle)  
**Relevant findings:** F08, F10

**Survey finding:** Respondents place perceived AI maturity in analysis and content, and its weakest areas in technical design, engineering, pattern development, sourcing and production. Around three quarters expect the share of consumer-facing creative output created or touched by AI to increase within two years.

**Argument in the article:** Fashion sees AI as more mature before product creation and after it: in analysis, then in content and marketing. The difficult middle is where ideas become physical products through technical disciplines. The article argues that the difference is not merely creative versus technical. It is the difference between work with a usable digital substrate and work that still depends on fragmented data, physical validation and tacit expertise.

**What remains uncertain:** Perceived maturity is not a capability benchmark, a forecast of adoption or a vendor comparison.

**Question to test:** Which proposed use cases have the data and validation path to be trusted, and which still depend on expertise or physical testing that AI cannot yet access?

## 5. Shaky optimism for an automated future

**Article section:** [Shaky optimism for an automated future](survey-analysis.md#shaky-optimism-for-an-automated-future)  
**Relevant findings:** F09, F11, F12

**Survey finding:** More than half are positive or optimistic about AI in fashion, while around one third remain uncertain. Around one quarter report AI-linked headcount reductions, hiring freezes or other cuts in their organisation. Around three quarters think AI could automate a third of their own role by 2030.

**Argument in the article:** Fashion is not treating AI as a passing fad. Optimism sits beside uncertainty, fear about decision quality and concern about how automation changes work. The article’s point is that broad enthusiasm does not settle the terms on which automation should enter a workflow, or who bears the consequence when it fails.

**What remains uncertain:** These are reported organisational experiences and beliefs about future automation, not causal evidence of AI-driven job loss or a forecast of employment outcomes.

**Question to test:** What expertise must remain able to challenge an AI-supported workflow, and who owns the decision when the result is wrong?

## 6. The non-virtuous loop

**Article section:** [The non-virtuous loop](survey-analysis.md#the-non-virtuous-loop)  
**Relevant findings:** F03, F05, F11

**Survey finding:** Only around one quarter trust AI for critical decisions, while around two thirds describe AI initiatives as sandboxed or only lightly connected to other systems.

**Argument in the article:** AI that cannot access the live data and systems where the answer resides will often produce generic or incomplete output. Users then trust it less, which makes organisations less willing to connect it to consequential workflows or systems. That keeps the tool at arm’s length and preserves the conditions that made it unhelpful.

**What remains uncertain:** The survey supports shallow integration and low trust. It does not prove that one causes the other, or that more access will make an AI system trustworthy. The loop is The Interline’s explanation and a hypothesis for a bounded test.

**Question to test:** Are we withholding the context an AI system would need to become useful because we do not yet trust it? If so, which controlled workflow could let it earn greater access through useful, reviewable results?

## How to use these arguments

The order is the essay’s order:

1. Fashion uses AI but does not trust it with important decisions.
2. Its AI estate is already fragmented.
3. Its spending grows before return is clear.
4. Perceived capability sits around, rather than through, product creation.
5. Optimism remains conditional because the terms of automation are unresolved.
6. The trust loop explains why more tools and more spend will not resolve the problem on their own.

For any application, return:

1. **Survey finding** — using only `approved-findings.md`;
2. **The Interline’s analysis** — drawing on the article section above;
3. **What remains uncertain**;
4. **What to inspect next**.

The companion is useful when it produces an inspectable question or bounded experiment. It is not useful when it produces a generic recommendation or a false maturity ranking.
